The gap this day is about

Between two censuses, most African offices have no subnational annual measure of economic activity.

Night-time lights do not fill that gap. But they are the only free, continental, district-level signal that updates every year.

Anchor this in the room's reality: ask how many offices have a subnational GDP series updated more often than every five years. Usually very few hands. That gap is the whole reason this day exists. Four properties make the signal worth the day: it is available every night since 2012 and free; it is consistent, the same sensor over the whole continent; it is fine-grained at about 500 m; and it is independent — not derived from the statistics you are trying to check. Do not oversell. The next two slides are deliberately about what the literature has, and has not, established.
1 min
The proposition, stated honestly
Not “light equals GDP”.

A spatially detailed signal that correlates with activity and infrastructure — whose relationship to your own official figures you can measure, and only then decide whether to use.

What you are actually deciding today Not whether night-time lights are good. Whether they are good enough, in your country, for one named question.
This distinction is the spine of the day. Every participant arrives with one of two priors — that satellite data is magic, or that it is unusable — and both are wrong in the same way: they treat it as a property of the data rather than of the data-plus-question-plus-country. The afternoon produces a correlation coefficient for their own territory. That number, not this slide, is what settles the argument for each office.
3 min

What the literature establishes — it works

Lights improve growth estimates where accounts are weak Where national income accounts are poor, the best growth estimate weights measured growth and light-predicted growth roughly equally. Henderson, Storeygard & Weil (2012), American Economic Review → A complement to your accounts, never a replacement.
Electrification is detectable, village by village Rural electrification in Senegal and Mali was detected against ground-truth records, at the level of individual settlements. Min, Gaba, Sarr & Agalassou (2013), Int. J. Remote Sensing → The most defensible use for an African NSO.
Sources · Henderson et al. 2012, 10.1257/aer.102.2.994 · Min et al. 2013, 10.1080/01431161.2013.833358. Full list at the end of this deck.
These two are why the day is worth holding. Henderson, Storeygard and Weil is the foundational economics result — note the phrasing carefully: a *composite*, giving lights roughly equal weight to the measured figure. Not a substitute. Min and colleagues is the one to dwell on with this audience. It is African, it is validated against ground records held by the utilities, and it addresses the question most offices in the room actually have a mandate for. The next slide is the other half, and it is the more important one.
2 min

…and it fails quietly

Correcting saturation reversed a published finding Once DMSP top-coding is corrected, primary cities in Sub-Saharan Africa grow faster than secondary cities — matching the census. Bluhm & Krause (2022), J. Development Economics → Saturation is not a footnote. It reverses conclusions.
Which dataset you choose changes the answer Economic results differ materially by lights product — and the differences are largest exactly where developing-country studies operate. Gibson, Olivia, Boe-Gibson & Li (2021), J. Development Economics → Name your product and version, every time.
Sources · Bluhm & Krause 2022, 10.1016/j.jdeveco.2022.102880 · Gibson et al. 2021, 10.1016/j.jdeveco.2020.102602.
If you have time for one finding in this whole deck, take Bluhm and Krause: a real, published, peer-reviewed result that *reversed* once a sensor artefact was corrected. Not a hypothetical failure — a documented one, in Sub-Saharan Africa, in a leading journal. That is the argument for spending the morning in the raster rather than in the output table. Both papers say the same thing in different registers: the number you compute is a function of choices you may not have noticed making.
Part 1

Where the data comes from

Two instruments, thirty years, and one discontinuity you must not ignore.

4 min

Thirty years, two instruments

The discontinuity DMSP-OLS reports an uncalibrated 6-bit digital number; VIIRS reports calibrated radiance. Never splice the two without an explicit harmonisation step — and if you do harmonise, say which method you used, because they disagree. Most published African NTL work starts in 2012 for exactly this reason, and so does this workshop.

The fourth row is the one that saves teams on the day: the Earth Engine route does the same job with nothing downloaded, which is the difference between finishing and not finishing for anyone on a constrained network. The trap in that row is real. Two offices can follow this deck faithfully, one via NASA and one via EOG, and publish annual figures that differ by a few percent. Neither is wrong. Both are wrong if they omit the product name.

Part 2

From radiance to an indicator

The five steps you will run this morning and this afternoon.

4 min

The pipeline, end to end

3 min

Three numbers, three different questions

1
Sum of Lights
Total radiance over an area.
How much lit activity? Dominated by cities.
2
Lit area
Share of territory above a threshold.
How far has light spread? The electrification question.
3
Spatial inequality
Gini of radiance across districts.
Concentrating, or spreading? The one nobody computes.
Compute all three, always They can move in opposite directions in the same country, in the same year — and that divergence is the finding.
Sum of Lights grows both when existing places get brighter and when new places light up; it cannot tell you which. Lit area is insensitive to how bright the capital became, which is exactly what you want when the question is electrification. The Gini answers a question neither of the others can. Spend the extra minute on inequality. It is usually the most policy-relevant of the three and almost nobody computes it — which makes it the cheapest way for an office to publish something genuinely new.
1 min
A number you must publish alongside
The threshold is a decision,
not a fact.

At 0.1 nW·cm⁻²·sr⁻¹ a country may be 40 % lit. At 5.0, six per cent. Same country, same year, same satellite.

The rule Publish the threshold next to the number. Every time, without exception.
This is the single most common way a night-time lights indicator becomes unreproducible — and it happens silently, because the number itself looks fine. Two offices can report "lit area" for the same country in the same year and differ by a factor of six, purely through an unstated parameter. Neither has made an error. Neither figure can be used. The same argument applies to the ADM level and the product version. This afternoon the notebook writes all three into the metadata sidecar automatically, so the participants do not have to remember.

That last line is the argument for the whole morning. Every one of these three studies produced a clean table that survived internal review — the error was upstream of the table, in the raster, where nobody looked. It is also why the exploration laboratory comes *before* the analysis laboratory, and why the artefact inventory is a graded deliverable rather than an optional extra. A team that skips it will still produce numbers today. They just will not know whether the numbers are theirs or the sensor's.
3 min

What NTL proxies well — and what it does not

Over-claiming

  • GDP in levels
  • Household connection rates
  • Daytime informal activity
  • Anything at village scale
  • Cross-country levels

Defensible

  • Electrification progress
  • Urbanisation and settlement growth
  • Crisis and conflict impact
  • Spatial inequality of infrastructure
  • District comparison within one year
Take the right-hand column one line at a time — each has a specific reason, and the reason is what participants need to repeat back to their management. GDP: correlated, not equal, and the elasticity differs by country and by decade. Connection rates: a household that is connected but cannot afford to use power emits no light. Informal activity: much of it happens in daylight. Village scale: 500 m pixels plus blooming make small settlements unreliable. Cross-country levels: lighting technology and lighting culture differ, so a level comparison measures both the economy and the streetlamps. Left column, one caveat worth adding aloud: crisis impact is the most robust of all, because an abrupt loss of light is unambiguous in a way growth never is.

3 min

Is my proxy publishable? A decision framework

The honest sentence you are aiming for "Night-time radiance at ADM2, VNP46A4 collection 002, lit threshold 0.5 nW·cm⁻²·sr⁻¹, correlates with our official electrification rate at r = 0.NN across NN districts in 20NN. Suitable for tracking relative change between districts; not suitable for estimating connection rates in absolute terms."
Say plainly that roughly half the teams should expect to land on "diagnostic only", and that this is the correct answer for their country rather than a poor performance. Offices under pressure to publish something new will be tempted to skip step 3 — the whole point of the afternoon is that step 3 is what separates a statistical product from a picture.
