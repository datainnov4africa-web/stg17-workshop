Talking to machines

Prompt engineering is specification writing — and this room has done it for thirty years

Day 2 · 09:00–10:15 · 75 minutes
STG17 Action Plan 2025–2030 · activities 3.3.2 and 4.1.1
Seventy-five minutes, and the longest talk of the week. Say at the outset what it is not: it is not a list of tricks, and there is no slide of magic phrases. The frame to establish in the first five minutes is that this audience already does the hard part of this work. Writing an instruction that a thousand enumerators will follow identically, in three languages, without you present, is a harder problem than prompting a model — and it is the same problem.
3 min
You have written one of these

An enumerator's instruction manual

A specification for a worker you will never meet, who cannot ask you what you meant, and whose output becomes an official statistic.

That is the whole skill State the task. Define the terms. Give worked examples, including the awkward ones. Say what to do when the case does not fit. Then test it on real material before you deploy it to a thousand people.
Ask for a show of hands: who has written or reviewed enumerator instructions, or a coding manual for occupation or industry? Most hands go up, and that is the point of the slide. Then draw the parallel explicitly. Every failure mode of a bad enumerator manual has an exact counterpart in a bad prompt: an undefined term, a missing edge case, an example that contradicts the rule, an instruction that assumes context the worker does not have. The one difference worth naming now: the model does not get tired, and it does not tell you when the instruction was unclear. It guesses, fluently, every time.
1 min
The reframing this talk rests on
A prompt is a measurement instrument.

Not a spell, not a knack, not a personal style. Something you specify, test, version and document — because the number that comes out of it will be attributed to your office.

Let this sit. It is the sentence the rest of the morning elaborates, and the one that changes how a methodologist treats the whole subject. The consequence, stated once here and returned to at the end: if a prompt is an instrument, then changing it is a methodological change. It goes in the documentation, it gets a version number, and a published figure names the version that produced it. That is not bureaucracy — it is the only way anyone reproduces your result, including you, next year.
Part 1 of 3

What actually moves the number

Two published results that contradict most of what circulates.

5 min

Two findings worth more than a hundred tips

Formatting alone moved accuracy by up to 76 points A separator, a space, the order of a field. The sensitivity persisted with larger models and more examples. Sclar, Choi, Tsvetkov & Suhr (2024), ICLR → Formatting is part of your method, not presentation.
“You are an expert…” did not improve anything Four model families, 2 410 factual questions: no improvement over adding none. Zheng, Pei, Logeswaran, Lee & Jurgens (2024), Findings of EMNLP → The most widespread technique has no measured benefit here.
Sources · Sclar et al. (2024), Quantifying Language Models' Sensitivity to Spurious Features in Prompt Design, ICLR, arXiv:2310.11324 · Zheng et al. (2024), When “A Helpful Assistant” Is Not Really Helpful, Findings of EMNLP, arXiv:2311.10054
These two are on the same slide deliberately, because together they say something uncomfortable: the thing everyone does has no measured effect, and the thing nobody documents has an enormous one. On the persona finding, be precise about its scope — it concerns objective, factual tasks, which is what a statistical office mostly has. Zheng and colleagues also found that the *best* persona per question does help, but that picking it automatically performs no better than random. So the honest reading is not "personas are useless", it is "you cannot know in advance which one helps, so it is not a technique you can specify". Someone will say their own experience contradicts this. The correct answer is: then measure it on your own task, and if you find an effect, report it. That is the entire posture of the morning.
1 min
Hold on to this number
76accuracy points

The gap between the best and worst meaning-preserving format of the same prompt, on the same model, for the same task.

What it means for a statistical office An undocumented prompt format is an undocumented instrument setting. Two colleagues can follow the same written method and publish materially different figures.
Pause on this. Seventy-six accuracy points is not a subtle effect, and it comes from changes a methodologist would not think worth recording — a colon instead of a dash, the order of two fields, whether a list uses hyphens or numbers. The parallel this room will feel: it is the difference between two enumerators reading the same question with different emphasis. We have decades of practice controlling exactly that in questionnaire design, and none of it has been applied to prompts yet. If anyone asks whether this holds for the largest commercial models: the study covers open models, and the authors report that the sensitivity persists as models get larger. Do not overclaim beyond what they tested — measure it on the model you actually use.
4 min

What has evidence, and what does not

TechniqueEvidenceUse it when
Worked examples in the promptStrong Always, for classification and extraction
Step-by-step reasoningStrong, task-dependent Arithmetic and multi-step logic; not for extraction
Fixed output formatStrong Always. It is also what makes output checkable
Repeat and take the majorityStrong, costs 3–5× When one answer is not enough to publish
“You are an expert…”None measured Harmless. Do not count on it, do not specify it
Sources · Brown et al. (2020), Language Models are Few-Shot Learners, NeurIPS · Wei et al. (2022), Chain-of-Thought Prompting, NeurIPS · Wang et al. (2023), Self-Consistency Improves Chain of Thought Reasoning, ICLR · Zheng et al. (2024), Findings of EMNLP.
Work down the table rather than reading it. The two rows that change behaviour in this room are the second and the last. Step-by-step reasoning is task-dependent, and the dependence matters: on extraction — "pull the total from this table" — asking for reasoning adds tokens, latency and an opportunity to talk itself out of the right answer. On arithmetic and multi-step logic it helps substantially. Match the technique to the task, not to fashion. Majority voting is the one nobody budgets for. Running the same prompt five times and taking the modal answer measurably improves reliability, and it costs five times as much. For a figure that will be published, that is often the right trade — and it is a decision to make explicitly, in advance.
The anatomy of a prompt that works

Five parts, in this order, every time

Spend real time here — three minutes, and point at parts three and five in turn. Part three is the specification. A rule stated in prose is interpreted; a worked example is imitated. This is exactly why coding manuals have example tables, and the same reason applies. Include the hard cases: examples that are all easy teach the model that the task is easy. Part five is what turns a conversation into a process. If the output shape is fixed, your code validates it — a missing field, a code outside the classification, a number where a string belongs. That check runs on every record, for free, and it catches things no human reviewer would notice on the four-hundredth row. Part four's delimiting matters more than it looks, and Part 3 of this deck explains why: an input that is not clearly separated from your instructions is an input that can rewrite them.
Part 2 of 3

Evaluation is your home ground

This is the part where a statistical office has an advantage over a software team.

4 min

Build the evaluation set first

1
Sample
Draw 100–200 real records at random. Not the interesting ones — at random, or your estimate is biased.
2
Label
A human assigns the correct answer. Two humans on a subset, so you know your own agreement rate.
3
Split
Half to develop the prompt against, half untouched. The untouched half is your only honest estimate.
4
Measure
One number, agreed in advance. Then change the prompt and measure again.
Say plainly that this is a sampling and measurement problem, and that everyone in this room already knows how to do it. That is not flattery — it is why an NSO can evaluate a prompt better than most software teams, who typically try three prompts and keep the one that looked best on two examples. Step two carries the point people miss: measure your *own* agreement rate first. If two experienced coders agree on only 88 per cent of occupation strings, a model at 85 per cent is close to human performance, and a target of 95 per cent is incoherent. Without that baseline you cannot tell whether a model is underperforming or the task is genuinely ambiguous. Step three is the one that gets skipped under time pressure, and skipping it is how an office reports a number that does not survive contact with next month's data.
3 min

What to measure, by task

TaskMeasureThe trap
Coding to a classificationAccuracy at the level you publish Four digits scores far below two
Extracting a figure from a documentExact match, then digits-only match Right number, wrong unit
Answering from a corpusCorrect answers and correct refusals Never refusing scores well
Drafting textA human rubric, applied blind A model scoring its own output
One number, chosen before you start Agree the metric before you see any results. Choosing it afterwards is choosing the metric your prompt happened to win on.
The first row is the one to dwell on with this audience, because it is the trap they will actually fall into. A model that codes occupation to two digits at 91 per cent may be at 68 per cent on four digits — and if you publish four digits, 91 is not your number. Measure at the level of publication. Row three connects back to yesterday's laboratory: an assistant evaluated only on questions the corpus answers will score beautifully and refuse nothing. Both sets are needed, and the refusal set is the one nobody builds. Row four's trap is worth naming out loud. Using a model to score another model's drafting is convenient and it is not evidence. There is a real literature on LLM-as-judge and its biases; if a team wants to go that way, they should read it first rather than assume it works.
3 min
A word that misleads

Temperature 0 is not reproducibility

It reduces variation. It does not promise the same output twice, and no provider offers that promise.

What reproducible actually requires The model name and its version, the exact prompt text, the parameters, and the date. Record all four beside any figure you publish — and expect the provider to retire that version while your series is still running.
This is the slide that changes procurement conversations, so do not rush it. An office building a monthly indicator on a hosted model is building on something the vendor may deprecate. When the version changes, your process is measuring with a different instrument, and the discontinuity is in your series whether or not you noticed. Two practical consequences. First, ask for version pinning in the contract, in writing — it is the fifth question on the Day 1 procurement slide. Second, keep your evaluation set and rerun it when a version changes: that turns an invisible break into a measured one, which is the difference between a documented revision and an unexplained jump. This is also the honest argument for open weights on anything feeding a publication: a file on your disk does not change.
Part 3 of 3

Three ways this goes wrong

Two are technical. The third is the one that ends a career.

4 min

Three failure modes

What happensWhyWhat stops it
The input rewrites your instructions Respondent text says “ignore the above…”, inside your prompt Delimit the input, and validate the output shape
The output drifts silently The provider updated the model Rerun the evaluation set on a schedule
Confidential text leaves the building A free-text field carried an identifier Classify the data before choosing where the model runs
The third one is not a technical failure It is a breach of statistical secrecy, and it is irreversible. Once the text has left, you cannot recall it and you cannot prove it was not retained.
On the first row: this is prompt injection, and a statistical office is unusually exposed because its inputs are text written by members of the public. An open comment field is untrusted input. Treat it the way you would treat text going into a database query. The second row is the quiet one. A prompt that scored 91 per cent in March can score 84 in September with no change on your side, and nothing will tell you. A scheduled rerun of the evaluation set is cheap insurance and almost nobody does it. Give the third row weight. A free-text occupation field saying "I am the only midwife in [village]" is identifying, and no de-identification step was ever applied to it because nobody classified it as microdata. Day 1's rule stands: classify the data before you choose where the model runs.
1 min
The line, restated for prompts
A prompt that produced a published figure
is part of the method.

It gets a version number, it goes in the documentation, and the figure names the version that produced it. If you cannot show the prompt, you cannot defend the number.

This is the absolute of the morning, and it follows from everything before it. If formatting alone can move accuracy by seventy-six points, then an undocumented prompt is an undocumented instrument, and a figure produced by one is not reproducible by anyone — including the person who produced it. The practical test to leave them with: could a colleague, given only your documentation, reproduce your figure to the digit? If not, the documentation is incomplete, and the missing part is usually the prompt. This is also the argument that gets prompt work taken seriously internally. Framed as "tuning the AI", it sounds like fiddling. Framed as instrument documentation, it is recognisably the office's own discipline.
2 min
Take this to your desk

What a documented prompt records

  • The exact text, whitespace included
  • Model name and version, and the date
  • Parameters, and whether answers were repeated and voted
  • The evaluation set, its size and how it was drawn
  • The score, and your own human agreement rate
  • What it is not suitable for
Tell them to photograph this. It is the artefact of the session, and it is short enough to become a template by Friday. The last line is the one that protects them. A prompt validated on occupation coding is not validated on industry coding, and someone will reuse it within a month. Writing down what it was not tested on is how that reuse gets caught in review rather than in a publication. The fifth line pairs the two numbers deliberately. A score of 87 per cent means nothing on its own; 87 against a human agreement rate of 89 means something quite specific, and it is the sentence a methodologist can defend in a committee.
