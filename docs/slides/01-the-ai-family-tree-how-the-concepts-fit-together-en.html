2 min

Why half an hour on vocabulary

Three sentences, all of which have appeared in real terms of reference at real statistical offices:

Seen in the wild "We need an AI to answer questions about our publications."
"The agent will use machine learning to classify the responses."
"We will fine-tune the model on our data so it knows our statistics."

Each describes a different system, with different cost, different risk, and a different answer to "who is accountable for the output". The third one, in particular, usually describes something the author did not intend and would not have funded.

The point of this half-hour Not to make you an engineer. To make sure that when your office writes a specification, procures a service, or explains a decision to a Board, the words in the document mean what the reader thinks they mean.

4 min

What a language model actually does

Read that last line twice A language model is not a knowledge base with a conversational front end. It is a next-token predictor. It produces the most plausible continuation, and plausibility and truth agree most of the time — which is exactly what makes the disagreements dangerous.
Sources · The architecture: Vaswani et al. (2017), Attention Is All You Need, NeurIPS. · Few-shot behaviour and scale: Brown et al. (2020), Language Models are Few-Shot Learners, NeurIPS. · Hallucination as a property rather than a bug: Ji et al. (2023), Survey of Hallucination in Natural Language Generation, ACM Computing Surveys 55(12).
This is the single most important slide of the week and it is worth going slowly. The room contains people who will later be asked "can we trust it?" — and the honest answer starts here: the model has no internal notion of true or false, only of likely. Everything on Day 2 about verification, and everything on Day 4 about validation, follows from this one fact. In the laboratory this afternoon participants print the actual probability distribution for a prompt of their own.
Part 2

Three ways to make it useful

Prompt, retrieve, retrain — in that order, always.

5 min

The three levers, and where each one acts

"We will fine-tune the model on our data so it knows our statistics" This is the sentence from slide 2, and it describes the wrong lever. Fine-tuning teaches a model to write like your office; it does not reliably teach it to know your numbers, and it gives you no way to cite a source. The requirement behind that sentence is almost always RAG — which is cheaper, faster, updatable the moment a figure is revised, and produces an answer you can trace back to a document.
Sources · RAG: Lewis et al. (2020), Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Knowledge-Intensive NLP Tasks, NeurIPS. · Low-cost fine-tuning: Hu et al. (2021), LoRA: Low-Rank Adaptation of Large Language Models. · Prompting technique: Wei et al. (2022), Chain-of-Thought Prompting Elicits Reasoning in Large Language Models, NeurIPS.
If you take one slide from this deck into a procurement meeting, take this one. The ordering — prompt, then retrieve, then retrain — is not a matter of taste; it is ordered by cost, by time, and by how easily the result can be audited. The amber warning is the correction most rooms need, and it is worth stating plainly: a vendor proposing fine-tuning as the answer to "the model should know our data" is proposing the expensive lever for a problem the cheap one solves better.
4 min

RAG, in the shape you will build this afternoon

Why a statistical office should care about step 3 specifically An answer that cites the paragraph it came from can be checked by a human in ten seconds. An answer with no source has to be trusted or re-derived. Only one of those two is compatible with publishing under an official mark.
Point at the amber line. The most common failure in a first RAG build is that the retriever returns nothing useful and the model, left with an empty context, falls back on its own memory and answers fluently and wrongly. This afternoon's laboratory deliberately includes questions the corpus cannot answer, so participants see that failure happen and learn to instruct the model to refuse.
Part 3

Agents, and where supervision goes

The step from "answers a question" to "takes an action".

5 min

Assistant → agent: one change, and it is not a small one

Assistant (what you build at 14:45)Agent (what you build at 15:45)
What it does Reads, then writes an answer Reads, then decides which tool to call, calls it, reads the result, repeats
Who chooses the steps You did, when you wrote the pipeline The model does, at run time
Failure looks like A wrong sentence A wrong sentence, or a wrong action, or a loop that will not stop
What it costs One model call As many calls as it decides to make
Where a human belongs Checking the output before publication Checking the output and approving actions before they happen
The word "tool" is doing a lot of work here A tool is any function you let the model call: a database query, a retrieval step, a file write, an email. The model does not run the tool — your code does, after the model asks for it. That gap is where every approval gate, audit log and safety check lives. Design it before you need it.
Standardising the tool interface · The Model Context Protocol (MCP) is an open protocol for connecting models to tools and data sources in a uniform way — so a tool written once can be reused across systems rather than re-implemented per vendor. modelcontextprotocol.io
The row that matters for this audience is the last one. In an assistant, a human checks the output. In an agent, a human must also stand between the decision and the action — and that is an architectural choice made at build time, not a policy you can add afterwards. Say plainly that "human in the loop" is not a slogan here; it is a specific place in the code, and this afternoon's laboratory shows participants exactly where it goes.

Where each concept belongs in the statistical value chain

StagePlausible useThe right leverNon-negotiable control
CollectionClassifying free-text survey answers; coding occupations Prompting, or a small trained classifierA labelled evaluation set — measured, not assumed
ProcessingExtracting tables from a PDF publication Prompting + structured outputVerification against the source. Day 2 morning
AnalysisWriting and debugging the analysis code itself PromptingThe code is read and run by a statistician
DisseminationAnswering public questions about your publications RAGEvery answer carries its source passage
DocumentationDrafting methodological notes and metadata Prompting, or RAG over past notesSigned off by the method owner
Estimation Producing an official figure directly from a model. Not on this list, and not on any list. A language model outputs the plausible, not the measured. Use it around your statistics, never in place of them.
There is an international framework for this The UNECE High-Level Group for the Modernisation of Official Statistics has published Machine Learning for Official Statistics, including a quality framework for choosing methods and guidance on moving from experiment to production. Your office does not have to invent its own criteria — and a proposal that ignores an existing international framework is easier to challenge than one that engages with it.
Sources · UNECE HLG-MOS, Machine Learning for Official Statistics — machine learning group project, ethical-use guidance and quality framework; statswiki.unece.org · UN Committee of Experts on Big Data and Data Science for Official Statistics — the UN Global Platform, revisited on Day 3.
The amber row is the one to dwell on. Every other row is an efficiency gain around an existing statistical process; that row would replace the process itself, and it is the line this workshop does not cross. Mention the UNECE framework explicitly — it gives participants something citable to bring back to a hesitant management, and it signals that this is an established methodological conversation rather than a novelty.
3 min

Eight words, fixed for the week

LLMA next-token predictor trained on a very large text corpus. Not a database.
TokenThe sub-word unit the model reads and writes. Cost and context limits are counted in these, not in words.
Prompt engineeringShaping the input. Free, instant, and the lever to exhaust first.
RAGShaping the context — retrieving your documents so the answer is grounded and citable.
Fine-tuningChanging the weights. Teaches style well, facts badly.
AgentA model allowed to choose which tools to call, and in what order. Your code still executes them.
MCPAn open protocol for exposing tools and data to models uniformly — write a tool once, reuse it.
HallucinationA fluent, confident, false output. A property of the mechanism, managed by verification — not a bug awaiting a patch.
The discipline this buys you When someone in your office says "the agent will use RAG to fine-tune the answers", you now know that sentence contains three terms and describes no coherent system — and you can ask the question that unblocks the project: which of the three levers is this, and what are we measuring it against?
Read the closing line as written; it is the one participants tend to quote back later in the week. Point them at the glossary on the workshop site, which carries these terms in both languages with the same wording as the notebooks — the point of the shared vocabulary is that it is genuinely shared, not repeated approximately in five places.
