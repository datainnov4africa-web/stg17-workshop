# THE STANDARD

I am not asking for acceptable slides. I am asking for a deck that would pass as
the work of a design studio commissioned by a development bank, and that a
minister would not be embarrassed to sit through.

Three things most generated decks fail, so check yourself against them:

  1. EVERY SLIDE HAS A VISUAL IDEA, not only a verbal one. A title with two
     paragraphs under it is a page, not a slide. If a slide's only content is
     prose, you have not finished designing it — give it a colour panel, a
     numeral, a card row or a table.

  2. THE DECK HAS RHYTHM. White, white, white, white reads as one long document
     and the audience stops noticing that anything changed. Never more than three
     consecutive white slides; aim for roughly a third of the deck carrying a
     full colour field.

  3. TYPE DOES THE WORK. One family, a wide size range, decisive weight
     contrast. A 46pt numeral beside 14pt supporting text carries more than any
     decoration. Nothing is centred except the title slide.

# VISUAL REGISTER — editorial-graphic

Flat colour fields, oversized numerals, strong typography, clean tables.

  NOT: stock photography, clip art, icon fonts, emoji, WordArt.
  NOT: gradients other than the title slide, text shadows, glows, 3-D, bevels.
  NOT: rounded-everything. Panels 10-14pt radius; full colour fields square.

It must read on a badly calibrated projector in a lit room, print legibly in
black and white, and not look dated in ten years.

# PALETTE — these seven values and no eighth

  NAVY  0B2545   structure, colour fields, headings, authority
  JADE  1B7A43   primary accent, the normal path, rules
  AMBER F2A900   the ONE element the eye should find; warnings
  INK   33403A   body text

  LINE  D5E6DF   hairlines and table borders

Amber is scarce by design: if two things on a slide are amber, neither is
emphasised. Never carry meaning by colour alone — a colour-blind viewer and a

<aside class="notes">

this room are the second and the last.

Step-by-step reasoning is task-dependent, and the dependence matters: on
extraction — "pull the total from this table" — asking for reasoning adds tokens,
latency and an opportunity to talk itself out of the right answer. On arithmetic
and multi-step logic it helps substantially. Match the technique to the task, not
to fashion.

Majority voting is the one nobody budgets for. Running the same prompt five times
and taking the modal answer measurably improves reliability, and it costs five
times as much. For a figure that will be published, that is often the right
trade — and it is a decision to make explicitly, in advance.
</aside>

<h2>Build the evaluation set first</h2>

<div class="steps">
<div><div class="i">1</div>
     <div class="t">Sample</div>
     <div class="d">Draw 100–200 real records at random. Not the interesting ones —

<div><div class="i">2</div>
     <div class="t">Label</div>
     <div class="d">A human assigns the correct answer. Two humans on a subset,
     so you know your own agreement rate.</div></div>
<div><div class="i">3</div>
     <div class="t">Split</div>
     <div class="d">Half to develop the prompt against, half untouched. The
     untouched half is your only honest estimate.</div></div>
<div><div class="i">4</div>

<aside class="notes">

this room already knows how to do it. That is not flattery — it is why an NSO can

and keep the one that looked best on two examples.

If two experienced coders agree on only 88 per cent of occupation strings, a model
at 85 per cent is close to human performance, and a target of 95 per cent is
incoherent. Without that baseline you cannot tell whether a model is
underperforming or the task is genuinely ambiguous.

Step three is the one that gets skipped under time pressure, and skipping it is how
an office reports a number that does not survive contact with next month's data.

The last line is the one that protects them. A prompt validated on occupation
coding is not validated on industry coding, and someone will reuse it within a
month. Writing down what it was not tested on is how that reuse gets caught in
review rather than in a publication.

The fifth line pairs the two numbers deliberately. A score of 87 per cent means
nothing on its own; 87 against a human agreement rate of 89 means something quite
specific, and it is the sentence a methodologist can defend in a committee.
</aside>
</section>
